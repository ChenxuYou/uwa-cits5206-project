Criterion 2 · Client communication and MVP agreement

One document, so there is one thing to sign

  • The client receives a single file: the scope summary, then five questions. Not a sixty-page specification nobody signs.
  • Part 1 states what we will build, what we are deliberately not building, and what we attempt only if there is time.
  • It reproduces the client's own worked example to the cent before any interface is written — that is how we prove the engine is right.
  • Part 2 carries the five questions, each with a default, so an unanswered question cannot hold up the confirmation.
  • Every promise in Part 1 traces back to a numbered requirement, so what we sign and what we build cannot drift apart.

The sign-off framework

Two confirmations, three dates, one sentence if nothing comes

  • We ask the client to confirm two things: the scope in Part 1, and the ownership position. Nothing else.
  • The document carries a sign-off block with a line for each, so agreement is a reply, not a negotiation.
  • Tue 18 Aug — the reply date we named. Wed 19 Aug — one chase, today. Fri 21 Aug — we stop waiting.
  • If nothing comes back we write the scope as our proposal, not an agreement, and say so in the report.

Review before sending

Nothing goes to the client unread by a second person

  • The scope summary and the five questions were reviewed line by line before they left the team.
  • The review is what cut the batch from six questions to five — a question that changes nothing in the product is not worth the client's time.

  • The cost of a second reader is an hour. The cost of an unclear question is a week of waiting for the wrong answer.

The Conflicts in the Client's Material

Owner — to be assigned before this deck is shown

19 August 2026

Criterion 1 · the problem, named precisely

The guide and the calculator do not agree with each other

  • The client gave us two authorities: a costing guide that states the method, and a calculator that implements it.
  • Worked through line by line, they diverge in several places, and some of the workbook's arithmetic looks simply wrong.
  • The clearest case: for commercial users the guide deducts no income before the 1.35 uplift; the calculator deducts federal and other income first.
  • Same platform, two different published prices. Our reading is that the guide governs — grant income should not subsidise a commercial rate.

Source: client costing guide Step 3 · client calculator, commercial row · docs/client/2026-08-15-scope-and-questions.md Q4.

19 August 2026

Tomorrow · what we ask the client

Ask, do not assume — this one changes a published price

  • Put the divergence to the client with the row, the column and the formula, so their answer is checkable rather than a matter of opinion.
  • Ask the general form of the question too: should the tool follow the policy, or reproduce figures already published from the workbook?
  • Offer the full defect list as a separate document. “Your spreadsheet is wrong” does not belong next to “please sign this”.
  • One formula changes if they say the calculator governs. Cheap now, expensive once the engine is written.

[ASK] If the client asks us to reproduce arithmetic we believe is wrong, is building it and recording that we raised it the right call?
